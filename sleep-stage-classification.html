Advances in Deep Learning for Automatic Sleep Stage Classification

A comprehensive survey of architectures, datasets, evaluation methods, and open challenges.

Abstract

Sleep stage classification is an important part of sleep research because it gives researchers and clinicians a way to describe how sleep changes over the course of a night. Traditionally, a trained scorer examines polysomnography recordings and assigns a stage to each short segment of the recording. This is valuable work, but it takes time, and two scorers do not always agree, especially on the boundaries between stages.

This review looks at how deep learning has been used to make that process more automatic. Rather than focusing only on reported accuracy, it considers what information different models learn, how they handle the sequence of sleep stages, which datasets they use, and how fairly they are evaluated. The discussion covers convolutional and recurrent networks, attention-based models, hybrid approaches, and methods that combine more than one physiological signal.

An important theme throughout the review is that strong results on a familiar benchmark do not necessarily translate to equally strong results on new people, devices, or clinical populations. The final sections therefore discuss class imbalance, generalization, interpretability, and the practical difficulties of using these systems outside a research setting.

1. Introduction

Sleep is not a single, uniform state. Across a typical night, the brain moves between lighter and deeper non-REM sleep and REM sleep, with transitions that vary from person to person. These patterns matter in research on sleep quality, neurological conditions, and many other aspects of health. To study them, researchers need a reliable description of when each sleep stage occurs.

In conventional sleep studies, recordings are divided into epochs, commonly 30 seconds long, and each epoch receives a stage label according to a scoring standard such as the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) rules. Scorers consider EEG patterns alongside eye movements and muscle activity. This produces a useful reference, but manual scoring can be slow, and some epochs are genuinely ambiguous.

Earlier automatic methods often relied on manually designed features, such as spectral power in different frequency bands. Deep learning changed the approach by allowing models to learn representations directly from signals or time-frequency images. This opened the door to models that can recognize local signal patterns and, increasingly, account for the stages that come before and after an epoch. The main question is no longer simply whether a model can classify sleep stages, but whether it can do so consistently across datasets and realistic conditions.

2. Background

2.1 Sleep Architecture and Staging Rules

Under the commonly used AASM system, sleep is described using five labels: Wake (W), N1, N2, N3, and REM. N1 is a transitional stage between wakefulness and more established sleep, while N3 represents deep non-REM sleep. REM sleep has its own characteristic combination of brain activity, eye movements, and muscle tone.

These categories are useful, but their boundaries are not always sharp. N1 is particularly difficult for automatic systems because it can resemble neighboring stages and is often less common than N2. That makes it important to look beyond overall accuracy: a model might classify the dominant stages well while still missing a substantial portion of N1 epochs.

2.2 Data Sources

Polysomnography, usually shortened to PSG, is the standard source of information for sleep staging. A PSG study can record EEG, electrooculography (EOG), electromyography (EMG), and additional physiological measurements such as breathing or oxygen saturation. Different studies use different subsets of these channels, so model inputs are not always directly comparable.

EEG-only approaches are attractive because they can reduce the number of sensors required. On the other hand, eye movement and muscle activity can provide information that is especially helpful for distinguishing REM sleep from other stages. Wearable devices introduce another trade-off: they are easier to use outside a laboratory, but they may record fewer channels or noisier signals. These differences matter when judging how transferable a model really is.

3. Datasets

Public sleep datasets have made it possible to compare methods and reproduce experiments, although comparisons still require care. Sleep-EDF is frequently used in methodological papers, while the Sleep Heart Health Study (SHHS) provides a larger cohort. MASS and ISRUC offer further opportunities to study different recordings, annotations, and participant groups.

The dataset itself is only part of the story. Results also depend on how participants are divided between training and testing, which channels are selected, how stages are mapped, and whether recordings are filtered or resampled. In particular, splitting epochs from the same participant across training and test sets can make performance appear more general than it really is. Subject-wise evaluation is therefore an important consideration.

Another limitation is that a model trained on one collection may struggle on a different collection because of changes in recording equipment, scoring conventions, participant characteristics, or clinical conditions. Testing across datasets helps reveal these weaknesses.

4. Deep Learning Models

4.1 Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)

Convolutional neural networks are well suited to finding local patterns in physiological signals. Depending on the design, a CNN may work directly on a one-dimensional EEG waveform or on a two-dimensional representation such as a spectrogram. It can learn recurring shapes and frequency-related features without requiring every feature to be specified by hand.

CNN-based systems are often relatively straightforward to train and can be efficient at inference. Their limitation is that a single epoch does not always contain enough information for a confident decision. A brief transition may make more sense when the model can also see what happened in the preceding and following epochs. This motivates architectures that add an explicit temporal component.

4.2 Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs)

Sleep stages follow a sequence rather than appearing as independent observations. Recurrent architectures, including LSTMs and GRUs, were introduced into sleep staging to capture that context. For example, an ambiguous epoch can be interpreted differently depending on whether it follows a long period of N2 sleep or a period of wakefulness.

Models such as L-SeqSleepNet illustrate the value of learning from neighboring epochs. The trade-off is additional computational cost and a more complicated training procedure. Recurrent networks also do not automatically solve generalization problems; they still depend on the quality and diversity of the data used to train them.

4.3 Transformers and Attention Mechanisms

Attention-based models provide another way to work with temporal context. Rather than processing a sequence only step by step, they can learn relationships among different positions in the input. In sleep staging, this can help a model combine information within an epoch with patterns that extend across several epochs.

Approaches discussed in the literature include SleepTransformer, SleePyCo, and CTCNet, although they differ in their exact objectives and architecture. Attention weights may offer useful clues about which parts of an input influenced a prediction, but they should not automatically be treated as a complete explanation of a model’s decision. Larger attention-based systems can also require substantial training data and computing resources.

4.4 Hybrid Architectures

Hybrid designs try to combine complementary strengths. A convolutional component can identify short signal patterns, while a recurrent or attention-based component considers how those patterns evolve over time. Other approaches use multiple physiological channels, allowing the model to learn from EEG together with EOG or EMG.

Some research also explores generative methods to address data imbalance, including approaches related to SleepEGAN. These methods are interesting because rare or difficult stages can be underrepresented, but synthetic examples need careful evaluation. An apparent improvement on one dataset does not guarantee that the generated samples reflect meaningful physiological variation.

4.5 Lightweight and Real-Time Models

Not every sleep staging application has access to a large computer or a full PSG setup. Wearable and home-monitoring devices have tighter limits on memory, power, and available sensors. This has motivated smaller neural networks, reduced input channels, and approaches designed for faster inference.

The practical challenge is to preserve useful performance while reducing complexity. A lightweight model should be evaluated not only by its parameter count, but also by how it behaves on noisy recordings, how much context it needs, and whether it can operate with the sensors available in a real device.

5. Preprocessing and Feature Engineering

Preprocessing choices influence what a model sees before learning begins. Some studies use raw EEG with relatively little transformation, while others filter the signal, remove artifacts, or convert it into frequency-based representations using tools such as the FFT, short-time Fourier transform, or wavelets. Each choice highlights some information while potentially discarding other information.

Artifact handling is especially relevant for recordings made outside a controlled laboratory. Movement, electrode problems, and other disturbances can resemble meaningful changes in the signal. For that reason, a robust pipeline needs consistent preprocessing and a clear account of which steps were applied to training and test data.

Self-supervised learning is another promising direction. Methods such as ContraWR and NeuroNet explore learning useful representations from recordings without requiring a manual sleep-stage label for every epoch. Such pretraining may help when annotated datasets are limited, but its value still needs to be tested on downstream tasks and unfamiliar data.

6. Quantitative Evaluation

Accuracy is easy to report, but it can hide important errors. Sleep-stage datasets are often imbalanced, with N2 much more common than N1. A model can therefore obtain a respectable overall accuracy while performing poorly on a stage that researchers care about. Macro-averaged F1-score, per-stage precision and recall, and Cohen’s kappa provide additional perspectives.

Confusion matrices are particularly useful because they show which stages are mistaken for one another. Confusion between Wake and N1, or between N1 and N2, may reflect the ambiguity of transitional sleep. It is also useful to examine performance across participants rather than reporting only one aggregate score.

For a fair comparison, papers should describe their data split, preprocessing, scoring standard, class mapping, and evaluation protocol. Differences in these choices can be large enough that two published numbers are not directly comparable. Cross-dataset evaluation and testing on recordings from unseen participants provide stronger evidence of generalization than a single within-dataset result.

7. Open Challenges and Future Directions

Class imbalance remains one of the most persistent problems, especially for N1. Weighted loss functions, sampling strategies, and data augmentation may help, but they should be judged by whether they improve recognition of difficult stages without creating new errors elsewhere.

Generalization is equally important. A model trained on one dataset may encounter different sensors, participant demographics, health conditions, and annotation practices when deployed elsewhere. More diverse training data and external validation are needed before a system can be considered reliable in a broader setting.

Interpretability also deserves careful attention. Clinicians and researchers may want to understand why a particular epoch received a label, especially when the prediction conflicts with an expert scorer. Visual explanations and attention analyses can support investigation, but they are not substitutes for clinical validation.

Finally, there is growing interest in self-supervised learning, multimodal fusion, privacy-preserving training such as federated learning, and smaller models suitable for home monitoring. These directions address different parts of the same problem: making sleep-stage classification not only accurate on a benchmark, but useful and dependable in practice.

8. Conclusion

Deep learning has expanded the range of methods available for automatic sleep staging. CNNs can learn useful local signal representations, while recurrent and attention-based models bring in the temporal context that makes sleep stages easier to interpret. Multimodal systems can use additional physiological information when it is available.

Even so, there is no single architecture that solves every difficulty. Performance depends on the dataset, recording setup, label quality, class distribution, and evaluation design. For future work, the most meaningful progress may come from better external validation, careful treatment of uncertain stages, and models that remain reliable when the recording conditions change.
